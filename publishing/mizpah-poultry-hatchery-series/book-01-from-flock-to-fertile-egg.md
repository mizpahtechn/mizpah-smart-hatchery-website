## Purpose of this book

A hatchery's results begin long before eggs enter an incubator. Fertile eggs are the outcome of a managed breeding flock: suitable male and female birds, sound nutrition, appropriate housing and environment, effective health and biosecurity, careful mating management, clean nests, disciplined egg collection and accurate records.

This book explains that upstream system. Its principal worked example is the managed chicken breeder flock, especially commercial parent stock, because this is the context in which much of the available breeder-management guidance is written. It also identifies important differences in indigenous chicken, layer-breeder, turkey, duck, goose, guinea-fowl and quail systems. Those differences are not interchangeable: mating behaviour, reproductive physiology, flock structure, artificial insemination practices and management targets can vary by species and genetic line. Where the evidence in this book is chicken-specific, it is labelled as such.

The book follows the breeder operation from programme design and bird selection through rearing, reproductive management, egg collection, traceability and performance review. It is intended to help readers ask better questions, keep better records and recognise when a decision requires current breeder-company guidance or veterinary advice. It is not a substitute for a current strain manual, a nutrition formulation, a veterinary prescription or a site-specific engineering design. Detailed egg grading, storage and incubation procedures are treated more fully in the companion books in this series.

## 1. The hatchery begins with the breeder flock

A commercial hatchery is often judged by the number of saleable chicks it produces. Yet the hatchery cannot manufacture fertility. Its starting material is the hatching egg, and the biological potential of that egg is strongly influenced by the breeding flock and the way eggs are handled before setting.

A breeder flock is a group of male and female birds managed to produce fertile eggs for incubation. It is not simply a table-egg flock with males added. Breeder management must consider reproduction, bird health and welfare, mating behaviour, body condition, nutrition, egg production, shell quality and the supply of eggs that meet the hatchery's requirements.

The production chain can be understood as:

**Breeder selection and rearing → mating and reproductive maturity → egg formation and laying → collection and selection → storage and transport → incubation → hatch and chick processing.**

Every stage affects the next. Poor flock uniformity can complicate reproductive management. Inadequate male condition or mating effectiveness can reduce fertility. Dirty nests and infrequent collection can increase contamination and damage. Weak records can conceal the point at which performance began to deteriorate.

The practical lesson is simple: hatchery performance is a chain of connected processes. Improving the incubator while ignoring the breeder farm may leave the most important causes untouched.

### 1.1 Different production systems require different breeder plans

Breeder management is not identical across all poultry species or production systems. Commercial broiler parent stock, layer breeder stock, indigenous chicken breeding groups, turkey breeders, duck breeders and smallholder village breeding flocks differ in genetics, body size, mating behaviour, housing, feed requirements and production objectives.

A breeder programme should therefore begin by defining:

- The species and strain or genetic type.
- The intended offspring market and performance objective.
- The source, health status and traceability of breeding birds.
- The expected production cycle and replacement strategy.
- The housing and mating system.
- The hatchery's egg specifications and setting schedule.
- The veterinary and biosecurity plan.

Use the management guide supplied for the specific strain or species. A target developed for one commercial broiler breeder genotype should not automatically be applied to a local chicken, turkey or duck flock.

### 1.2 Define the product before planning the flock

The breeder operation should know what it is expected to supply. A hatchery producing broiler chicks may require a different breeder programme from one producing layer chicks, indigenous dual-purpose birds, turkey poults or ducklings.

Define the product in operational terms:

- Species and strain.
- Expected chick type and customer market.
- Target number of saleable chicks per week or batch.
- Required egg size or weight range, where specified by the breeder programme.
- Egg cleanliness and shell-quality criteria.
- Traceability and health documentation.
- Delivery frequency and geographic market.

This definition informs flock size, breeder sourcing, feed planning, housing, labour and the number of eggs that must be produced and set.

## 2. Understanding fertility and hatchability

Fertility and hatchability are related but different measurements. Confusing them can lead a farm or hatchery to treat the wrong problem.

**Fertility** describes whether an egg was fertilised. In hatchery analysis, fertility is commonly estimated by examining eggs or breakout samples and identifying evidence of embryonic development, with appropriate procedures for distinguishing infertile eggs from very early embryo deaths.

**Hatchability of eggs set** is the proportion of all eggs placed in the incubator that produce saleable or counted chicks, according to the hatchery's stated definition.

**Hatchability of fertile eggs** is the proportion of fertile eggs that hatch. This measure helps separate a fertility problem from losses occurring after fertilisation.

A hatchery should state the numerator and denominator every time it reports a percentage. The terms “hatch rate” and “hatchability” are sometimes used differently by farms, hatcheries and suppliers. Always document the exact calculation.

### 2.1 Core calculations

**Fertility percentage**

\[
\text{Fertility \%}=\frac{\text{Fertile eggs identified}}{\text{Eggs examined}}\times100
\]

**Hatchability of eggs set**

\[
\text{Hatchability of eggs set \%}=\frac{\text{Chicks hatched}}{\text{Eggs set}}\times100
\]

**Hatchability of fertile eggs**

\[
\text{Hatchability of fertile eggs \%}=\frac{\text{Chicks hatched}}{\text{Fertile eggs identified}}\times100
\]

*Example:* A batch contains 1,000 eggs set. Breakout and hatch records identify 900 as fertile, and 810 chicks hatch. Fertility is 90%. Hatchability of eggs set is 81%. Hatchability of fertile eggs is 90%.

The example is arithmetic only, not a universal performance target. Targets vary with species, strain, flock age, egg handling, storage, incubation equipment and the hatchery's definition of a successful chick.

### 2.2 Why the distinction matters

If fertility is low but hatchability of fertile eggs is strong, the investigation should begin upstream: breeder males, mating effectiveness, flock condition, reproductive maturity, sex ratio, health and management.

If fertility is acceptable but hatchability of fertile eggs is low, investigate egg handling and storage, incubation conditions, sanitation, embryo mortality and hatchery procedures. The diagnostic process should use breakout findings and batch records rather than guesswork.

## 3. Choosing and managing breeding stock

The genetic and health quality of the breeding birds sets the starting potential of the operation. Source breeding stock from reputable suppliers with appropriate health, vaccination and traceability information. Confirm that the birds are suitable for the intended production system and that the supplier's documentation matches the delivered flock.

Before placement, prepare a written flock plan covering:

- Stocking numbers and sex composition according to the relevant breeder guide.
- House preparation, cleaning, disinfection and downtime.
- Feed and water availability.
- Brooding and rearing arrangements.
- Lighting and environmental controls.
- Veterinary oversight and vaccination programme.
- Identification, weighing and record-keeping.
- Staff responsibilities and biosecurity entry rules.

Do not improvise a sex ratio or breeding age from a generic online table. The correct approach depends on species, strain, mating system, bird age, body condition, fertility history and breeder-company recommendations.

### 3.1 Rearing determines later reproductive readiness

The reproductive performance of a breeder flock is influenced by how birds are reared. Growth, body-weight development, flock uniformity, skeletal development, health, nutrition and management during the rearing period affect the transition into reproductive maturity.

Monitor birds consistently using the measurement schedule recommended for the strain. Record representative body weights, uniformity, feed intake, water use, mortality and health observations. A single average can hide uneven groups; the spread of body weights and the proportion of birds near the target are also useful.

When results deviate from the breeder programme, investigate the cause and seek qualified technical advice before making major feed, lighting or management changes.

## 4. Male management and mating effectiveness

Males contribute half of the genetic material and are essential to flock fertility. Their presence alone does not guarantee effective mating. Physical condition, leg and foot health, mobility, body weight, social behaviour, maturity and the flock's mating environment all matter.

A practical male-management routine includes:

1. Observe male activity and distribution within the house.
2. Check mobility, feet, legs, body condition and signs of injury.
3. Monitor body weight and condition against the specific breeder guide.
4. Watch for excessive aggression, poor mating activity or birds being excluded from feed and water.
5. Record male mortality, removals, replacements and any changes to the sex composition.
6. Investigate fertility changes by age group and house rather than relying only on a whole-farm average.

Poor male condition can reduce mating effectiveness. Overly aggressive or unsuitable males can also injure females and create welfare problems. Any adjustment to male numbers or replacement strategy should follow the strain-specific programme and be documented.

## 5. Female condition, nutrition and egg production

Female reproductive performance depends on more than the quantity of feed delivered. Body condition, nutrient balance, water availability, environmental comfort, flock health and the timing of management changes all influence egg production and egg quality.

Feed should be formulated and managed for the species, strain, age and stage of production. Work with a poultry nutritionist or qualified feed specialist when formulating diets or correcting deficiencies. Avoid using a generic ration as a substitute for a breeder-specific nutrition programme.

Monitor:

- Daily egg numbers and hen-day production.
- Egg weight distribution, where relevant.
- Feed delivery and feed clean-up patterns.
- Water consumption and water quality.
- Body weight and body condition.
- Mortality, morbidity and veterinary findings.
- Shell defects and rejected egg categories.

A sudden change in egg production, egg weight, water use or feed clean-up can be an early warning. Record the date, affected house, scale of the change and any recent changes in feed, lighting, temperature, water, staff routine or health status.

## 6. Housing, nests, lighting and the laying environment

The breeder house should support bird welfare, normal mating behaviour, reliable access to feed and water, clean egg production and effective staff movement. Ventilation, temperature, litter condition, stocking density, lighting, nest design and maintenance all contribute to the result.

Nest boxes should be introduced and managed according to the breeder programme. Birds need to learn to use nests; poorly managed nest access can increase floor eggs. Keep nest interiors and egg belts clean. Inspect for broken equipment, sharp edges, wet or dirty litter and obstructions that may damage eggs or injure birds.

Lighting programmes are strain- and system-specific. Timing, intensity and photoperiod affect reproductive development. Do not copy a lighting schedule from a different strain or production system. Use the current breeder guide and document all changes.

## 7. Egg collection and first-line handling

Egg collection is both a quality-control activity and a biosecurity control. Eggs left in nests or on the floor for long periods are more exposed to damage, contamination and temperature variation.

Aviagen's Ross Parent Stock guidance describes frequent collection and clean nests and belts as important practices. Its published guidance recommends a minimum of four nest-egg collections daily for the system described, with collection intervals adjusted to laying patterns. This is a breeder-management reference, not a universal rule for every farm; adapt the schedule to the species, housing design, egg volume and applicable breeder programme.

A sound collection routine should:

- Collect eggs at planned intervals, with more frequent attention during peak laying periods.
- Keep nest eggs and floor eggs identifiable and separate.
- Use clean hands and clean, suitable trays.
- Avoid rough handling, shaking and impacts.
- Keep collection equipment clean and maintained.
- Record the number of eggs collected and the categories rejected.
- Move eggs promptly to the designated egg handling area.
- Keep visitors and unrelated equipment out of egg rooms.

Floor eggs should be handled under a separate, documented policy because their contamination and damage risks may differ from clean nest eggs. Do not silently mix them with nest eggs and lose traceability.

### 7.1 Separate collection from final grading

The collection team should identify obvious damage and contamination, but final acceptance criteria should be written and consistently applied by trained personnel. The companion book *The Hatching Egg Selection Code* will cover shell defects, egg shape, size, cleanliness, cracks, double-yolk eggs and other grading decisions in detail.

Record rejected eggs by category. A rising number of dirty eggs, floor eggs or cracked eggs may indicate a problem with nests, litter, bird behaviour, collection timing, equipment or staff technique. Rejection records are management data, not just waste figures.

## 8. Farm-to-hatchery records and performance indicators

A breeder farm should be able to trace each egg batch to its flock, house, collection date and relevant handling history. The hatchery should be able to link the setting batch and hatch results back to that source.

Minimum useful records include:

| Record | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Flock identity, strain and placement date | Identifies the source and age of the flock |
| Male and female numbers | Tracks sex composition and changes |
| Daily egg production | Reveals production trends and sudden changes |
| Nest and floor egg counts | Highlights housing and collection issues |
| Rejected egg categories | Identifies quality losses and their causes |
| Feed and water records | Helps detect management or health changes |
| Mortality and veterinary events | Connects health events with production changes |
| Collection and dispatch batches | Preserves traceability |
| Hatchery fertility and hatch results | Connects breeder performance with downstream outcomes |

A record is only useful if it is complete, timely and reviewed. Assign responsibility for entering and checking each record. Use consistent units and definitions. Never compare percentages if the underlying denominators or definitions differ.

## 9. Common causes of poor fertile-egg supply

The table below is a starting diagnostic guide. It is not a substitute for veterinary investigation or a full hatchery breakout analysis.

| Observed problem | Possible areas to investigate |
|---|---|
| Falling fertility | Male condition, mating activity, flock age, sex composition, health, reproductive maturity |
| Rising floor eggs | Nest access, nest training, litter, house layout, collection timing |
| More cracked eggs | Nest or belt damage, handling technique, crowding, collection frequency |
| More dirty eggs | Nest hygiene, litter quality, floor-egg proportion, collection and staff practices |
| Falling egg production | Feed, water, body condition, lighting, heat stress, disease, management changes |
| Uneven egg size | Flock uniformity, age, nutrition, strain-specific production factors |
| Fertility differs between houses | House-level male condition, bird distribution, environment, health and management |

Use a structured investigation:
1. Confirm that the observation is real and the records are comparable.
2. Identify the affected flock, house, age group and dates.
3. Check for recent changes and equipment faults.
4. Compare with unaffected houses or earlier batches.
5. Review veterinary and breeder technical advice.
6. Make one controlled, documented correction where possible.
7. Monitor the result and record whether it improved.

Avoid making several major changes at once. If multiple variables are changed simultaneously, it becomes difficult to identify which action helped or caused harm.

## 10. A practical breeder-farm operating routine

### Daily
- Observe bird distribution, behaviour, mobility and general condition.
- Check feed and water availability and investigate unusual consumption.
- Collect and count eggs at planned intervals.
- Separate floor eggs and record rejected categories.
- Check nests, belts, trays and egg-room cleanliness.
- Record mortality, unusual events and maintenance issues.
- Confirm that collected batches are correctly identified and transferred.

### Weekly
- Review egg production and rejected-egg trends by house.
- Review body-weight and condition measurements according to the breeder programme.
- Check male condition and mating observations.
- Review feed and water records.
- Inspect nest equipment, belts, ventilation and lighting.
- Review biosecurity compliance and staff training.
- Compare breeder-farm records with hatchery feedback.

### At every batch dispatch
- Confirm flock and house identity.
- Record collection dates and batch numbers.
- Record quantities and any agreed quality categories.
- Confirm packaging and transport arrangements.
- Record dispatch time, vehicle and receiving hatchery.
- Retain a copy of the dispatch record.

## 12. Glossary

- **Breeder flock:** Male and female birds managed to produce fertile eggs for incubation.
- **Fertility:** The proportion of examined eggs identified as fertilised under the stated assessment method.
- **Hatching egg:** An egg intended and accepted for incubation.
- **Hen-day production:** A measure of egg production that relates eggs produced to the number of live hens present on the day.
- **Hatchability of eggs set:** Chicks hatched divided by eggs placed in the incubator, multiplied by 100.
- **Hatchability of fertile eggs:** Chicks hatched divided by fertile eggs, multiplied by 100.
- **Parent stock:** Breeding birds that produce offspring for a commercial production chain.
- **Floor egg:** An egg laid outside the designated nest area.
- **Traceability:** The ability to identify the source and handling history of an egg batch.

## 13. The biology behind a fertile hatching egg

A hatching egg is the outcome of a reproductive process involving both the female and male. Understanding the broad biology helps farm teams recognise why bird condition, mating effectiveness and egg handling matter.

### 13.1 The hen's reproductive system

In the domestic hen, the functional ovary releases the yolk (ovum). As it moves through the oviduct, albumen, shell membranes and shell are added in sequence. Fertilisation, when it occurs, takes place in the upper part of the oviduct shortly after ovulation. The egg then continues through the reproductive tract before laying.

The shell is not a protective coating that can be repaired after laying. Cracks and severe shell defects can compromise the egg's suitability for incubation. Internal egg quality is also influenced by bird health, nutrition, age and management before the egg is laid.

### 13.2 The male's contribution

Sperm are produced in the testes and transferred to the female during successful mating. Hens can store sperm in specialised storage sites in the reproductive tract, allowing fertilisation of subsequent ova after mating. The duration and effectiveness of sperm storage vary; a farm should not use one assumed interval as proof that a female remains fertile.

Monitor actual fertility results rather than assuming that the presence of males guarantees fertilisation. Changes in male activity, health, body condition, age distribution, mating behaviour or flock composition should be compared with fertility trends.

### 13.3 Fertility is not embryo survival

Fertilisation is the beginning of embryonic development, not a guarantee of a live chick. An egg can be fertilised and the embryo can die early, midway through incubation or close to hatch. If a hatchery classifies every egg without a visible embryo as infertile, early embryonic death may be misclassified and the breeder farm may be blamed incorrectly.

Breakout analysis must use a consistent method and trained personnel. Where the distinction is uncertain, record the finding as uncertain or seek a second assessment rather than forcing it into a convenient category.

### 13.1 The reproductive sequence in the hen

In the chicken, reproductive function involves coordinated activity of the ovary, oviduct and endocrine system. A mature follicle releases the ovum at ovulation. The infundibulum receives it, and fertilisation normally takes place in this region if viable sperm are present. As the egg moves through the oviduct, albumen, shell membranes and shell are added in sequence before the egg is laid. This means that the egg collected from a nest is the result of a biological process that began before ovulation and continued during passage through the oviduct.

The rooster's testes produce spermatozoa. During mating, semen is transferred into the hen's reproductive tract. Sperm that pass the selection and transport barriers can enter specialised sperm-storage tubules, particularly at the uterovaginal junction. Stored sperm may be released over time and travel towards the site of fertilisation. This storage mechanism helps explain why a single successful mating can contribute to fertility in more than one subsequently laid egg, although the duration and level of sustained fertility vary with species, line, hen condition, sperm quality and management. It is not sound practice to convert a general biological description into a fixed number of days for every flock. [11, 12, 13]

### 13.2 Why mating does not guarantee fertilisation

For fertilisation to occur, several events must succeed: the male must produce functional sperm; mating or insemination must transfer semen; sperm must move through the female tract; a sufficient population must enter and remain functional in storage sites; sperm must be released and reach the infundibulum; and viable sperm must interact successfully with the ovum. A failure at any point can reduce fertility.

This is why simply counting males or observing occasional mating is not enough to prove reproductive effectiveness. Male mobility, semen quality, female reproductive condition, social behaviour, flock environment and the timing of management events all contribute. Specialist semen tests can provide information about sperm characteristics, but laboratory measures should be interpreted by trained personnel and do not replace flock-level fertility records. [12, 13]

### 13.3 Fertility is not the same as embryo survival

An egg may be fertilised and still fail to produce a chick. Embryonic development can stop at an early, middle or late stage. When eggs are examined after incubation, the operator must distinguish true infertility from very early embryonic death as carefully as the available method allows. Misclassification can make a breeder flock appear to have a fertility problem when the underlying issue is embryo survival, egg handling or incubation.

For this reason, a hatchery should record its examination method, sample size, batch identity, examiner training and classification categories. Where results are uncertain, report the uncertainty instead of forcing every questionable egg into an infertile category. The breakout findings should be read together with egg storage history, incubation records, hatch timing and chick quality.

### 13.4 Species differences matter

Chicken reproductive anatomy provides a useful starting model, but poultry species should not be assumed to have identical reproductive behaviour or management requirements. Artificial insemination is a routine component of some commercial turkey systems, while natural mating is widely used in chicken breeder flocks. Ducks, geese, guinea fowl and quail have their own behavioural, anatomical and production characteristics. Even within a species, genetic lines and production objectives can change the practical management programme.

The 2024 review of sperm-storage structures across Galliformes cautions against assuming that structures and mechanisms are identical across all bird species simply because they share a broad anatomical description. The practical implication is to use species-specific literature and breeder guidance when planning a flock, and to label chicken-based examples clearly. [14]

## 14. Breeding programme design: match the system to the objective

A breeder flock should be designed around a defined production objective, not simply around the number of birds that can fit in a house.

### 14.1 Pure-line, parent-stock and farm breeding

Commercial poultry supply chains may involve several genetic multiplication stages. A commercial hatchery commonly receives eggs from a parent-stock flock, but smallholder and local breeding systems may use a simpler arrangement. The terms used by suppliers matter: ask whether birds are commercial stock, parent stock, grandparent stock, indigenous breeding birds or mixed-origin birds.

Do not advertise offspring as a particular commercial strain unless the source and breeding status support that claim. Maintain supplier records, delivery documentation and flock identity to protect traceability and customer confidence.

### 14.2 Natural mating and artificial insemination

Natural mating is widely used in many chicken breeder systems. Other species and specialised breeding programmes may use artificial insemination. The choice depends on species, genetics, scale, housing, labour skills, welfare considerations and technical support.

Artificial insemination requires trained operators, hygienic equipment, semen handling procedures and a species-specific protocol. It should not be improvised from a general chicken instruction for use in turkeys, ducks or other birds. Farms considering it should obtain training and veterinary or breeding-specialist oversight.

### 14.3 Flock identity and replacement planning

Every breeder flock should have a unique identity. At minimum, record source, strain or type, hatch date or age, placement date, house, sex composition, health documentation and any movement between locations. Replacement birds should be introduced under a documented health and biosecurity procedure.

Mixing birds of unknown age or source can make fertility analysis and disease investigation difficult. It can also compromise the accuracy of claims made to chick buyers.

### 14.1 Start with the breeding objective

A breeding programme should be designed backwards from the offspring required by the customer. A farm supplying commercial broiler chicks, replacement layer pullets, indigenous dual-purpose chicks, turkey poults or ducklings is not producing the same biological or commercial product. The objective determines the genetic stock, breeding method, production timetable, egg specifications, health controls and hatchery relationship.

Before acquiring birds, write a short production brief that answers:
- Which species and genetic type will be produced?
- Is the objective commercial performance, local adaptation, conservation of a breed, household production or a combination?
- Who will buy the offspring, and what characteristics do they require?
- What number and frequency of hatching eggs can the farm realistically supply?
- Which hatchery will receive the eggs, and what acceptance criteria does it apply?
- What veterinary, feed, housing, labour, transport and record systems are available?
- What is the contingency if the breeder flock underperforms or the hatchery cannot receive a batch?

A clear production brief reduces the risk of purchasing birds that do not fit the intended market or the farm's technical capacity.

### 14.2 Commercial parent stock and independent breeding groups

Commercial parent-stock systems are usually part of a structured genetic pyramid. The breeder farm receives a defined genetic line and follows the relevant company's programme for rearing, body-weight control, lighting, mating, nutrition and production. The purpose is to produce offspring with the expected commercial characteristics while maintaining breeder performance and health.

An independent or smallholder breeding group may use locally adapted birds and select replacements within the flock. Such a programme requires clear selection goals and disciplined records. If birds are selected only because they are large or lay many eggs, the farm may unintentionally neglect fertility, hatchability, chick survival, temperament, disease resilience or adaptation to local conditions. Selection should consider the whole production objective, not one visible trait.

### 14.3 Plan flock size from the egg requirement

Flock size should be calculated from a realistic production plan, not from an assumed universal number of eggs per hen. The manager should use the appropriate breeder performance objectives for the selected stock and then account for the farm's own expected rejection, fertility, hatchability and saleable-chick rates.

A planning model can be written as:

**Required eggs set = required saleable chicks ÷ expected saleable-chick rate per egg set.**

If the hatchery needs 8,000 saleable chicks and its planning assumption is 0.78 saleable chicks per egg set, the arithmetic requirement is approximately 10,257 eggs set. This is a planning illustration only; 0.78 is not a universal benchmark. The farm must use its own validated historical data or the relevant breeder/hatchery performance guidance, and should add an appropriate operational buffer only after considering storage, rejection and capacity constraints.

The breeder farm then estimates the number of females and males needed using strain- and age-specific production data. It should also plan for mortality, culling, replacement timing, seasonal variation, downtime and the fact that egg production is not constant throughout a flock cycle.

### 14.4 Build the supply chain before placement

A breeder flock should not be placed without a credible route for its eggs and offspring. Confirm the receiving hatchery's species capability, setting capacity, egg acceptance rules, collection schedule, transport arrangements, batch documentation and payment terms. If the farm operates its own hatchery, confirm that equipment, staff, backup power, sanitation, maintenance and chick marketing arrangements are ready.

The production plan should include a response if the hatchery changes its schedule, the road becomes impassable, power is unavailable, feed supply is interrupted or demand falls. A biologically successful breeder flock can still become an unprofitable operation if eggs cannot be collected, transported, set or sold at the right time.

## 15. Male and female management as one reproductive system

Male and female management should be evaluated together. A flock can have apparently healthy females but poor fertility if males are underconditioned, injured, excessively heavy, socially excluded or not mating effectively. Conversely, excessive male aggression can injure females and damage welfare.

### 15.1 Observe behaviour, not only numbers

A weekly head count cannot show whether mating is taking place effectively. Staff should observe flock behaviour at suitable times and record patterns such as:
- males that are inactive or consistently isolated;
- males with impaired walking, foot lesions or injuries;
- repeated aggression or avoidance;
- females with signs of injury or feather damage;
- uneven distribution of birds around feeders, drinkers or nests;
- changes in mating activity after management adjustments.

Observation must be carried out calmly and consistently. A single brief visit may not represent the flock's normal behaviour.

### 15.2 Sex composition is not a universal fixed ratio

A ratio copied from another farm may be unsuitable because the correct programme depends on species, genotype, age, mating method, housing and male condition. Follow the current breeder guide and record any planned changes. Do not add males simply because fertility has fallen until other causes—including male quality, maturity, health and mating effectiveness—have been assessed.

### 15.3 Separate male and female monitoring

Record male and female body weights separately where the breeder programme requires it. Track mortality, removals, culls, replacements and the number of active males. Review these records alongside fertility by flock age and house. This allows the manager to distinguish a broad flock problem from one concentrated in a particular house or age group.

### 15.4 Reproductive ageing and fertility trends

Fertility should be interpreted in relation to flock age and the production curve. Male reproductive performance can change as birds age; body condition, health, leg function, semen characteristics and mating behaviour may all contribute. Research reviews describe reproductive ageing in male broiler breeders and discuss management approaches, but their findings must be interpreted within the genotype and production system studied. They do not establish a universal age at which every male flock will decline. [15]

A manager should compare fertility trends with male and female age, body-weight records, removals, replacements, health events, environmental conditions and hatchery sampling methods. If fertility falls after a management change, establish the timing and affected houses before concluding that age alone is responsible.

### 15.5 Natural mating and artificial insemination

Natural mating is common in many chicken breeder systems. Artificial insemination is used in some other poultry sectors and specialised breeding programmes, including commercial turkey production. It requires trained personnel, suitable semen collection and handling, controlled timing, hygiene, equipment and a species-appropriate protocol.

Do not attempt to transfer an insemination interval, semen dose or handling method from one species or production system to another. Poor technique can injure birds, compromise welfare, introduce infection or produce unreliable fertility. The procedure should be designed and supervised by qualified specialists under applicable animal-welfare and veterinary requirements.

### 15.6 Investigate fertility in a structured sequence

When fertility declines, the investigation should move from data quality to flock biology and then to the wider production system:

1. Confirm the result: check the batch identity, sample size, examination method and denominator.
2. Locate the pattern: compare houses, flock ages, collection dates and male groups.
3. Review the males: body condition, mobility, feet, injuries, mortality, removals and mating behaviour.
4. Review the females: body condition, health, reproductive stage and any management changes.
5. Review the environment: heat load, ventilation, lighting, water access and equipment faults.
6. Review handling: collection, storage, transport and time to setting.
7. Review hatchery classification: separate infertility from early embryo death as reliably as possible.
8. Consult the breeder technical adviser and veterinarian before making major flock interventions.

The sequence is intended to organise investigation, not to diagnose a specific disease or prescribe a management change.

## 16. Nutrition, water and environmental management

Nutrition supports maintenance, growth, reproduction and egg formation. Breeder diets must be appropriate to species, strain, age and production stage. The quantity and composition of feed, feeding access, body condition and water availability should be monitored as a connected system.

### 16.1 Avoid generic feed prescriptions

A feed formula or daily allocation designed for one strain or stage should not be transferred to another flock without technical review. Energy, amino acids, minerals, vitamins and other nutrients have different roles, and the correct formulation depends on the production system and feed ingredients.

For farms mixing their own feed, quality assurance becomes especially important. Use reliable ingredient specifications, accurate weighing, appropriate mixing equipment and periodic checks. Investigate ingredient changes, storage conditions, mould, rancidity and contamination. Consult a poultry nutritionist when correcting deficiencies or reformulating diets.

### 16.2 Water is a production input

Water must be available in sufficient quantity and at suitable quality. Monitor water consumption, line function, drinker height and cleanliness, leaks and water source. Sudden changes in consumption may reflect equipment failure, temperature changes, feed changes or health problems. Test water quality when source, taste, colour, odour or flock performance raises concern.

### 16.3 Heat stress and tropical production

In hot climates, heat load can reduce feed intake, alter water consumption, affect bird behaviour and compromise production. The actual response depends on humidity, air speed, stocking density, house design, bird age and acclimatisation. A farm in a humid tropical region should not assume that the same cooling strategy used in a dry climate will work equally well.

Practical controls include maintaining ventilation systems and backup power; checking that air inlets and fans are not obstructed; providing reliable clean drinking water; monitoring bird distribution and panting; avoiding unnecessary handling during the hottest periods; maintaining shade and appropriate building insulation where applicable; and preparing an emergency response for fan or power failure.

These are general management considerations, not a substitute for a locally designed ventilation and heat-stress plan.

### 16.4 Feed quality assurance and mycotoxin risk

Feed ingredients can vary in nutrient content and physical quality. Poor storage, moisture ingress, pests, mould growth, rancidity, ingredient substitution and inaccurate weighing can undermine a ration that was correctly formulated on paper. Establish supplier specifications, receiving checks, storage rotation, batch identification and a procedure for rejecting suspect ingredients. Where contamination is suspected, use an appropriate laboratory and nutrition/veterinary advice rather than relying on smell or appearance alone.

Mycotoxin exposure is a feed-safety concern in many climates and supply chains. Risk depends on the ingredient, fungal species, toxin, concentration, duration of exposure and bird age or physiological state. Do not assume that one binder or additive makes contaminated feed safe. Prevention through sourcing, drying, storage and testing is preferable to relying on an unverified corrective product.

### 16.5 Heat load is more than air temperature

Birds respond to the combined effects of air temperature, relative humidity, air speed, radiant heat, stocking density, feather cover, age and acclimatisation. A thermometer reading by itself does not describe the entire heat load. Staff should observe bird behaviour and distribution as well as instrument readings, and should know the normal operating range for their specific house and equipment.

For tropical and humid environments, the farm's plan should include reliable power for ventilation, tested backup systems, alarm escalation, water availability and a written response to fan or controller failure. A generator that exists but has not been load-tested or cannot start automatically may not provide meaningful protection. Any cooling or ventilation design should be checked by a competent poultry-house engineer or specialist.

### 16.6 Changes to feed and water should be documented

When feed formulation, ingredient supplier, delivery schedule, water source, medication or supplementation changes, record the effective date and affected flock. This allows a manager to compare performance before and after the change. Avoid simultaneous unrecorded changes that make it impossible to identify the cause of a production response. Veterinary medicines and additives must be used only under their approved label, prescription and local regulatory requirements.

## 17. Biosecurity and health protection at breeder-farm level

A breeder farm supplies the next stage of the poultry production chain. A disease event can affect bird welfare, fertility, egg quality, hatchery operations and customer confidence. Biosecurity should be a daily system rather than a sign at the gate.

### 17.1 Build a written biosecurity plan

The plan should identify:
- the person responsible for biosecurity;
- approved entry points and visitor authorisation;
- vehicle and equipment movement controls;
- staff clothing, footwear and hand hygiene;
- cleaning and disinfection procedures;
- pest and wild-bird control;
- feed and water protection;
- carcass and waste disposal;
- flock separation and age-group management;
- disease reporting and veterinary escalation;
- procedures for receiving new birds and returning equipment.

The correct disinfectant, concentration, contact time and application method depend on the product label, target organism, surface and local regulations. Never mix chemicals unless the manufacturer explicitly permits it. Staff should use required personal protective equipment and follow safety data instructions.

### 17.2 Vaccination and veterinary decisions

Vaccination programmes are specific to location, disease risk, flock source, species, production system and applicable regulation. They should be developed or approved by a poultry veterinarian. This book does not prescribe a universal vaccine schedule or medication programme.

Record vaccine name, batch or lot, expiry, administration date, route, responsible person and any observed reaction. Keep veterinary prescriptions and treatment records. Observe withdrawal periods and legal restrictions where applicable, even when eggs are intended for hatching rather than human consumption.

### 17.3 Responding to a suspected disease event

If unusual mortality, respiratory signs, neurological signs, sudden production decline or other concerning symptoms occur:
1. Protect people and birds; avoid unnecessary movement.
2. Contact the flock veterinarian or relevant animal-health authority promptly.
3. Follow official reporting and sampling instructions.
4. Restrict visitors, equipment and bird movement as directed.
5. Preserve accurate mortality, production and movement records.
6. Do not sell or move birds or eggs in a way that could spread disease contrary to veterinary or regulatory advice.

Do not diagnose a disease from a single symptom or use unprescribed antibiotics as a substitute for investigation.

### 17.4 Biosecurity is a chain of controlled movements

A breeder farm should map how people, birds, vehicles, feed, trays, crates, tools, waste and carcasses move through the site. Each movement can introduce or spread pathogens. The written plan should assign responsibility for entry approval, cleaning, disinfection, pest control, isolation and record keeping.

A practical biosecurity audit should ask:
- Can an unauthorised visitor enter a poultry house?
- Are farm-only footwear and clothing available and used correctly?
- Are clean and dirty equipment routes separated?
- Are egg trays and transport containers cleaned under a validated procedure?
- Are dead birds removed and disposed of safely and promptly?
- Are rodents, wild birds and insects monitored?
- Are sick birds or unusual mortality reported immediately?
- Are staff trained to report a breach without hiding it?

The answers should be checked through observation and records, not only through a signed policy. Biosecurity procedures must be realistic enough that staff can follow them every day.

## 18. Egg collection, identification and the farm-to-hatchery handover

Collection staff are the first quality checkpoint. Their task is to preserve identity and prevent avoidable damage or contamination before eggs reach the grading and storage team.

### 18.1 Use a traceable batch code

A useful batch code should identify the farm, flock or house, collection date and sequence. The format is not important as long as it is unique, legible and used consistently.

The code should follow the eggs through collection, storage, transport, receiving, setting and hatch reporting. If eggs from different flocks or collection dates are combined, record the combination explicitly. Never rely on a worker's memory to reconstruct the source later.

### 18.2 Handover information

The dispatch note should include supplier and farm identity; flock and house identity; species and strain/type; collection date or date range; quantity and tray count; separate counts for any categories handled under a distinct policy; dispatch date and time; vehicle or carrier details where relevant; destination hatchery; responsible person; and unusual events or handling notes.

The receiving hatchery should acknowledge quantities and report discrepancies. A simple signed handover can prevent disputes and help identify where losses occurred.

### 18.3 Transport is part of egg management

Eggs should be protected from shock, vibration, direct sun, rain, contamination and uncontrolled temperature changes during loading and transport. Packaging, vehicle conditions, journey time and road quality all matter. The appropriate temperature and humidity targets belong in the egg-storage and handling book and must be based on species, storage duration and current technical references.

For long or rough journeys, evaluate packaging and route conditions. Avoid placing trays where they can slide, fall or be struck by other cargo. Clean and disinfect reusable transport equipment under a written procedure.

### 18.4 Define the point of responsibility for every loss

Eggs may be lost or rejected at collection, farm grading, storage, loading, transport, hatchery receipt, hatchery grading or setting. If the farm and hatchery use different definitions, disputes become difficult to resolve. Agree in writing how each quantity is counted and who records it.

A batch reconciliation should distinguish:
- eggs collected at the farm;
- eggs rejected at farm grading, by reason;
- eggs loaded and dispatched;
- eggs received by the hatchery;
- eggs rejected at receipt or hatchery grading;
- eggs accepted and set;
- eggs identified as infertile or showing embryo mortality;
- chicks hatched;
- chicks graded as saleable under the agreed specification.

This chain makes it possible to investigate the stage at which a loss occurred. It also prevents a farm from being blamed for damage that happened in transit or a hatchery from being blamed for eggs that arrived outside the agreed specification.

## 19. Performance review: turn records into management decisions

Records become valuable when managers review them and take proportionate action. A weekly dashboard should show trends by flock, house and batch, not only a farm-wide total.

### 19.1 Useful indicators

| Indicator | Suggested definition / calculation |
|---|---|
| Hen-day egg production | Eggs produced in the period divided by live hens present for that day, multiplied by 100; define whether floor and rejected eggs are included |
| Eggs per hen housed | Total eggs produced over a defined period divided by original number of hens placed |
| Rejection rate | Eggs rejected under the farm's stated criteria divided by eggs assessed, multiplied by 100 |
| Fertility | Fertile eggs identified divided by eggs examined, multiplied by 100 |
| Hatchability of eggs set | Chicks hatched divided by eggs set, multiplied by 100 |
| Hatchability of fertile eggs | Chicks hatched divided by fertile eggs identified, multiplied by 100 |
| Saleable chick rate | Saleable chicks divided by eggs set, multiplied by 100, if this is the agreed definition |

Do not compare farms or hatcheries unless definitions, sampling methods and time periods are comparable. State whether figures are based on all eggs, nest eggs only, eggs set, fertile eggs, or saleable chicks.

### 19.2 Use a trend, not a single number

A single day's fertility or egg production can be affected by sampling, recording errors or unusual events. Look for sustained changes and compare like with like: same strain, similar flock age, comparable season, similar egg storage and incubation conditions. Where possible, compare houses within the same farm and batches from the same source.

### 19.3 A simple monthly review meeting

The farm manager, breeder supervisor, hatchery representative and veterinarian or technical adviser should review egg production and rejection trends; fertility and hatchability feedback; male and female condition; mortality and health events; feed and water records; environmental and equipment issues; customer complaints or chick-quality feedback; and corrective actions.

Record decisions, responsible persons and due dates. Review unfinished actions at the next meeting.

### 19.4 Use a balanced dashboard

A useful breeder-farm dashboard should not reward one result while hiding damage elsewhere. For example, a high egg count may be accompanied by more floor eggs, more cracks or poorer hatchability. Review a balanced set of measures:

| Area | Example measure | Management question |
|---|---|---|
| Flock status | Live males and females by house | Is the flock composition known and current? |
| Production | Eggs laid per day and per hen | Is production following the expected curve? |
| Egg quality | Nest eggs, floor eggs and rejects by reason | Where are usable eggs being lost? |
| Health | Mortality, morbidity and veterinary events | Are health events linked to production changes? |
| Inputs | Feed delivery, feed use and water use | Are inputs consistent with the flock plan? |
| Reproduction | Fertility and hatchability using stated definitions | Is the problem before or after fertilisation? |
| Logistics | Eggs dispatched, received and accepted | Where do quantity differences occur? |
| Corrective action | Open actions, owner and due date | Are problems being resolved and checked? |

Managers should resist ranking farms or houses using a single percentage without checking the underlying definitions and production context.

## 20. Worked farm-to-hatchery example

The following example demonstrates how records can reveal where a problem may sit. All figures are illustrative and are not industry targets.

A breeder farm dispatches 4,000 eggs from one identified flock over a documented collection period. The hatchery receives 3,960 eggs in acceptable condition after recording 40 damaged eggs at receipt. Of the 3,960 accepted eggs, 3,800 are set after the hatchery's own grading. The hatchery later records 3,420 chicks hatched.

The farm and hatchery should reconcile dispatched quantity against received quantity; received quantity against eggs accepted and eggs set; egg rejection categories and timing; source flock and house identity; storage and transport history; fertility sampling method and sample size; and incubation and hatch records.

This example demonstrates why each stage needs its own denominator. The difference between eggs dispatched, received, accepted and set is operationally important. A fertility sample should not be treated as an exact count of every egg unless the entire batch has been examined using an appropriate method.

### 20.1 Worked calculation with explicit denominators

Assume a hypothetical batch has 4,000 eggs dispatched. The hatchery records 3,960 received, 3,800 accepted and set, and 3,420 chicks hatched. The hatchability of eggs set is:

3,420 ÷ 3,800 × 100 = 90%.

This calculation does not establish fertility. To estimate fertility, the hatchery must examine an appropriate sample or the entire batch using a suitable method and report the number examined and classification method. If 950 of a sample of 1,000 are classified as fertile, the estimated fertility in that sample is 95%; it does not mean exactly 3,610 of the 3,800 eggs in the whole batch were fertile unless the sampling design supports that inference.

If the hatchery reports 90% hatchability of eggs set and 95% estimated fertility, it should not automatically divide or combine those percentages without explaining the assumptions. The sample may not represent the entire batch, and early embryo deaths may be misclassified. The report should preserve the raw counts, definitions and method so a specialist can interpret the result.

### 20.2 A practical reconciliation question

If 4,000 eggs were dispatched but only 3,960 were received, the immediate difference is 40 eggs. That difference should be investigated as a transport or handover discrepancy. It should not be recorded as infertility, because no fertility examination has yet taken place. Likewise, eggs rejected at hatchery grading should be recorded separately from eggs that were set and failed to hatch.

## 22. Case study: fertility falls in one house

A farm operates several breeder houses. Hatchery feedback shows a decline in fertility from eggs sourced from one house, while other houses remain broadly stable. The manager initially proposes changing the male ratio across the entire farm.

A better response is to isolate the affected house and compare male and female numbers and age profile; male body condition, mobility and mating behaviour; recent male removals or introductions; feed and water access; house temperature, ventilation and lighting records; flock health and veterinary events; egg collection and batch identification; and hatchery sampling and breakout records.

If the evidence points to a house-specific male-management issue, a targeted intervention can be planned with the breeder technical adviser. If the evidence instead shows a sampling or traceability error, the farm avoids an unnecessary flock-wide change. The lesson is to diagnose at the smallest reliable level before changing the whole operation.

## 23. Case study: more floor eggs and dirty eggs

A farm observes a rising number of floor eggs and dirty eggs. Staff suggest that the flock has become less productive. The manager separates production volume from egg quality and reviews the records.

The investigation finds that the increase coincided with a damaged nest section and a change in litter condition. The farm repairs the nest equipment, restores the agreed litter-management routine, retrains collection staff and records nest versus floor eggs separately. The result is assessed over subsequent comparable periods rather than declared successful after one day.

The case illustrates how operational records can connect an observed egg-quality problem with a practical housing or workflow cause.

## 24. Reader's action plan

Before the next breeder-farm review, the reader should be able to answer:

1. Can every hatching-egg batch be traced to a specific flock and house?
2. Is the breeder strain or genetic type known and supported by supplier documentation?
3. Are current strain-specific management guides available to staff?
4. Are male and female condition monitored separately where required?
5. Are feed, water, health, mortality and egg-production records complete?
6. Are nest eggs and floor eggs recorded separately?
7. Are rejected eggs categorised rather than recorded as one unexplained total?
8. Does the hatchery report fertility and hatchability using clear definitions?
9. Are transport and handover records complete?
10. Are corrective actions assigned, dated and reviewed?

A breeder farm does not need expensive software to begin improving control. It needs consistent definitions, reliable records, trained staff and management that responds to evidence.
