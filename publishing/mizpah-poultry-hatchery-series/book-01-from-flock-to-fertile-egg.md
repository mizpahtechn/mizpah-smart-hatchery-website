## Purpose of this book

A hatchery's results begin long before eggs enter an incubator. Fertile eggs are the outcome of a managed breeding flock: suitable male and female birds, sound nutrition, appropriate housing and environment, effective health and biosecurity, careful mating management, clean nests, disciplined egg collection and accurate records.

This book explains that upstream system. It focuses on how a breeder flock produces hatching eggs and how farm decisions affect the quantity, fertility and usable quality of eggs delivered to the hatchery. Detailed egg grading and storage procedures are treated more fully in the companion books in this series.

## 1. The hatchery begins with the breeder flock

A commercial hatchery is often judged by the number of saleable chicks it produces. Yet the hatchery cannot manufacture fertility. Its starting material is the hatching egg, and the biological potential of that egg is strongly influenced by the breeding flock and the way eggs are handled before setting.

A breeder flock is a group of male and female birds managed to produce fertile eggs for incubation. It is not simply a table-egg flock with males added. Breeder management must consider reproduction, bird health and welfare, mating behaviour, body condition, nutrition, egg production, shell quality and the supply of eggs that meet the hatchery's requirements.

The production chain can be understood as:

**Breeder selection and rearing → mating and reproductive maturity → egg formation and laying → collection and selection → storage and transport → incubation → hatch and chick processing.**

Every stage affects the next. Poor flock uniformity can complicate reproductive management. Inadequate male condition or mating effectiveness can reduce fertility. Dirty nests and infrequent collection can increase contamination and damage. Weak records can conceal the point at which performance began to deteriorate.

The practical lesson is simple: hatchery performance is a chain of connected processes. Improving the incubator while ignoring the breeder farm may leave the most important causes untouched.

### 1.1 Different production systems require different breeder plans

Breeder management is not identical across all poultry species or production systems. Commercial broiler parent stock, layer breeder stock, indigenous chicken breeding groups, turkey breeders, duck breeders and smallholder village breeding flocks differ in genetics, body size, mating behaviour, housing, feed requirements and production objectives.

A breeder programme should therefore begin by defining:

- The species and strain or genetic type.
- The intended offspring market and performance objective.
- The source, health status and traceability of breeding birds.
- The expected production cycle and replacement strategy.
- The housing and mating system.
- The hatchery's egg specifications and setting schedule.
- The veterinary and biosecurity plan.

Use the management guide supplied for the specific strain or species. A target developed for one commercial broiler breeder genotype should not automatically be applied to a local chicken, turkey or duck flock.

### 1.2 Define the product before planning the flock

The breeder operation should know what it is expected to supply. A hatchery producing broiler chicks may require a different breeder programme from one producing layer chicks, indigenous dual-purpose birds, turkey poults or ducklings.

Define the product in operational terms:

- Species and strain.
- Expected chick type and customer market.
- Target number of saleable chicks per week or batch.
- Required egg size or weight range, where specified by the breeder programme.
- Egg cleanliness and shell-quality criteria.
- Traceability and health documentation.
- Delivery frequency and geographic market.

This definition informs flock size, breeder sourcing, feed planning, housing, labour and the number of eggs that must be produced and set.

## 2. Understanding fertility and hatchability

Fertility and hatchability are related but different measurements. Confusing them can lead a farm or hatchery to treat the wrong problem.

**Fertility** describes whether an egg was fertilised. In hatchery analysis, fertility is commonly estimated by examining eggs or breakout samples and identifying evidence of embryonic development, with appropriate procedures for distinguishing infertile eggs from very early embryo deaths.

**Hatchability of eggs set** is the proportion of all eggs placed in the incubator that produce saleable or counted chicks, according to the hatchery's stated definition.

**Hatchability of fertile eggs** is the proportion of fertile eggs that hatch. This measure helps separate a fertility problem from losses occurring after fertilisation.

A hatchery should state the numerator and denominator every time it reports a percentage. The terms “hatch rate” and “hatchability” are sometimes used differently by farms, hatcheries and suppliers. Always document the exact calculation.

### 2.1 Core calculations

**Fertility percentage**

\[
\text{Fertility \%}=\frac{\text{Fertile eggs identified}}{\text{Eggs examined}}\times100
\]

**Hatchability of eggs set**

\[
\text{Hatchability of eggs set \%}=\frac{\text{Chicks hatched}}{\text{Eggs set}}\times100
\]

**Hatchability of fertile eggs**

\[
\text{Hatchability of fertile eggs \%}=\frac{\text{Chicks hatched}}{\text{Fertile eggs identified}}\times100
\]

*Example:* A batch contains 1,000 eggs set. Breakout and hatch records identify 900 as fertile, and 810 chicks hatch. Fertility is 90%. Hatchability of eggs set is 81%. Hatchability of fertile eggs is 90%.

The example is arithmetic only, not a universal performance target. Targets vary with species, strain, flock age, egg handling, storage, incubation equipment and the hatchery's definition of a successful chick.

### 2.2 Why the distinction matters

If fertility is low but hatchability of fertile eggs is strong, the investigation should begin upstream: breeder males, mating effectiveness, flock condition, reproductive maturity, sex ratio, health and management.

If fertility is acceptable but hatchability of fertile eggs is low, investigate egg handling and storage, incubation conditions, sanitation, embryo mortality and hatchery procedures. The diagnostic process should use breakout findings and batch records rather than guesswork.

## 3. Choosing and managing breeding stock

The genetic and health quality of the breeding birds sets the starting potential of the operation. Source breeding stock from reputable suppliers with appropriate health, vaccination and traceability information. Confirm that the birds are suitable for the intended production system and that the supplier's documentation matches the delivered flock.

Before placement, prepare a written flock plan covering:

- Stocking numbers and sex composition according to the relevant breeder guide.
- House preparation, cleaning, disinfection and downtime.
- Feed and water availability.
- Brooding and rearing arrangements.
- Lighting and environmental controls.
- Veterinary oversight and vaccination programme.
- Identification, weighing and record-keeping.
- Staff responsibilities and biosecurity entry rules.

Do not improvise a sex ratio or breeding age from a generic online table. The correct approach depends on species, strain, mating system, bird age, body condition, fertility history and breeder-company recommendations.

### 3.1 Rearing determines later reproductive readiness

The reproductive performance of a breeder flock is influenced by how birds are reared. Growth, body-weight development, flock uniformity, skeletal development, health, nutrition and management during the rearing period affect the transition into reproductive maturity.

Monitor birds consistently using the measurement schedule recommended for the strain. Record representative body weights, uniformity, feed intake, water use, mortality and health observations. A single average can hide uneven groups; the spread of body weights and the proportion of birds near the target are also useful.

When results deviate from the breeder programme, investigate the cause and seek qualified technical advice before making major feed, lighting or management changes.

## 4. Male management and mating effectiveness

Males contribute half of the genetic material and are essential to flock fertility. Their presence alone does not guarantee effective mating. Physical condition, leg and foot health, mobility, body weight, social behaviour, maturity and the flock's mating environment all matter.

A practical male-management routine includes:

1. Observe male activity and distribution within the house.
2. Check mobility, feet, legs, body condition and signs of injury.
3. Monitor body weight and condition against the specific breeder guide.
4. Watch for excessive aggression, poor mating activity or birds being excluded from feed and water.
5. Record male mortality, removals, replacements and any changes to the sex composition.
6. Investigate fertility changes by age group and house rather than relying only on a whole-farm average.

Poor male condition can reduce mating effectiveness. Overly aggressive or unsuitable males can also injure females and create welfare problems. Any adjustment to male numbers or replacement strategy should follow the strain-specific programme and be documented.

## 5. Female condition, nutrition and egg production

Female reproductive performance depends on more than the quantity of feed delivered. Body condition, nutrient balance, water availability, environmental comfort, flock health and the timing of management changes all influence egg production and egg quality.

Feed should be formulated and managed for the species, strain, age and stage of production. Work with a poultry nutritionist or qualified feed specialist when formulating diets or correcting deficiencies. Avoid using a generic ration as a substitute for a breeder-specific nutrition programme.

Monitor:

- Daily egg numbers and hen-day production.
- Egg weight distribution, where relevant.
- Feed delivery and feed clean-up patterns.
- Water consumption and water quality.
- Body weight and body condition.
- Mortality, morbidity and veterinary findings.
- Shell defects and rejected egg categories.

A sudden change in egg production, egg weight, water use or feed clean-up can be an early warning. Record the date, affected house, scale of the change and any recent changes in feed, lighting, temperature, water, staff routine or health status.

## 6. Housing, nests, lighting and the laying environment

The breeder house should support bird welfare, normal mating behaviour, reliable access to feed and water, clean egg production and effective staff movement. Ventilation, temperature, litter condition, stocking density, lighting, nest design and maintenance all contribute to the result.

Nest boxes should be introduced and managed according to the breeder programme. Birds need to learn to use nests; poorly managed nest access can increase floor eggs. Keep nest interiors and egg belts clean. Inspect for broken equipment, sharp edges, wet or dirty litter and obstructions that may damage eggs or injure birds.

Lighting programmes are strain- and system-specific. Timing, intensity and photoperiod affect reproductive development. Do not copy a lighting schedule from a different strain or production system. Use the current breeder guide and document all changes.

## 7. Egg collection and first-line handling

Egg collection is both a quality-control activity and a biosecurity control. Eggs left in nests or on the floor for long periods are more exposed to damage, contamination and temperature variation.

Aviagen's Ross Parent Stock guidance describes frequent collection and clean nests and belts as important practices. Its published guidance recommends a minimum of four nest-egg collections daily for the system described, with collection intervals adjusted to laying patterns. This is a breeder-management reference, not a universal rule for every farm; adapt the schedule to the species, housing design, egg volume and applicable breeder programme.

A sound collection routine should:

- Collect eggs at planned intervals, with more frequent attention during peak laying periods.
- Keep nest eggs and floor eggs identifiable and separate.
- Use clean hands and clean, suitable trays.
- Avoid rough handling, shaking and impacts.
- Keep collection equipment clean and maintained.
- Record the number of eggs collected and the categories rejected.
- Move eggs promptly to the designated egg handling area.
- Keep visitors and unrelated equipment out of egg rooms.

Floor eggs should be handled under a separate, documented policy because their contamination and damage risks may differ from clean nest eggs. Do not silently mix them with nest eggs and lose traceability.

### 7.1 Separate collection from final grading

The collection team should identify obvious damage and contamination, but final acceptance criteria should be written and consistently applied by trained personnel. The companion book *The Hatching Egg Selection Code* will cover shell defects, egg shape, size, cleanliness, cracks, double-yolk eggs and other grading decisions in detail.

Record rejected eggs by category. A rising number of dirty eggs, floor eggs or cracked eggs may indicate a problem with nests, litter, bird behaviour, collection timing, equipment or staff technique. Rejection records are management data, not just waste figures.

## 8. Farm-to-hatchery records and performance indicators

A breeder farm should be able to trace each egg batch to its flock, house, collection date and relevant handling history. The hatchery should be able to link the setting batch and hatch results back to that source.

Minimum useful records include:

| Record | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Flock identity, strain and placement date | Identifies the source and age of the flock |
| Male and female numbers | Tracks sex composition and changes |
| Daily egg production | Reveals production trends and sudden changes |
| Nest and floor egg counts | Highlights housing and collection issues |
| Rejected egg categories | Identifies quality losses and their causes |
| Feed and water records | Helps detect management or health changes |
| Mortality and veterinary events | Connects health events with production changes |
| Collection and dispatch batches | Preserves traceability |
| Hatchery fertility and hatch results | Connects breeder performance with downstream outcomes |

A record is only useful if it is complete, timely and reviewed. Assign responsibility for entering and checking each record. Use consistent units and definitions. Never compare percentages if the underlying denominators or definitions differ.

## 9. Common causes of poor fertile-egg supply

The table below is a starting diagnostic guide. It is not a substitute for veterinary investigation or a full hatchery breakout analysis.

| Observed problem | Possible areas to investigate |
|---|---|
| Falling fertility | Male condition, mating activity, flock age, sex composition, health, reproductive maturity |
| Rising floor eggs | Nest access, nest training, litter, house layout, collection timing |
| More cracked eggs | Nest or belt damage, handling technique, crowding, collection frequency |
| More dirty eggs | Nest hygiene, litter quality, floor-egg proportion, collection and staff practices |
| Falling egg production | Feed, water, body condition, lighting, heat stress, disease, management changes |
| Uneven egg size | Flock uniformity, age, nutrition, strain-specific production factors |
| Fertility differs between houses | House-level male condition, bird distribution, environment, health and management |

Use a structured investigation:
1. Confirm that the observation is real and the records are comparable.
2. Identify the affected flock, house, age group and dates.
3. Check for recent changes and equipment faults.
4. Compare with unaffected houses or earlier batches.
5. Review veterinary and breeder technical advice.
6. Make one controlled, documented correction where possible.
7. Monitor the result and record whether it improved.

Avoid making several major changes at once. If multiple variables are changed simultaneously, it becomes difficult to identify which action helped or caused harm.

## 10. A practical breeder-farm operating routine

### Daily
- Observe bird distribution, behaviour, mobility and general condition.
- Check feed and water availability and investigate unusual consumption.
- Collect and count eggs at planned intervals.
- Separate floor eggs and record rejected categories.
- Check nests, belts, trays and egg-room cleanliness.
- Record mortality, unusual events and maintenance issues.
- Confirm that collected batches are correctly identified and transferred.

### Weekly
- Review egg production and rejected-egg trends by house.
- Review body-weight and condition measurements according to the breeder programme.
- Check male condition and mating observations.
- Review feed and water records.
- Inspect nest equipment, belts, ventilation and lighting.
- Review biosecurity compliance and staff training.
- Compare breeder-farm records with hatchery feedback.

### At every batch dispatch
- Confirm flock and house identity.
- Record collection dates and batch numbers.
- Record quantities and any agreed quality categories.
- Confirm packaging and transport arrangements.
- Record dispatch time, vehicle and receiving hatchery.
- Retain a copy of the dispatch record.

## 12. Glossary

- **Breeder flock:** Male and female birds managed to produce fertile eggs for incubation.
- **Fertility:** The proportion of examined eggs identified as fertilised under the stated assessment method.
- **Hatching egg:** An egg intended and accepted for incubation.
- **Hen-day production:** A measure of egg production that relates eggs produced to the number of live hens present on the day.
- **Hatchability of eggs set:** Chicks hatched divided by eggs placed in the incubator, multiplied by 100.
- **Hatchability of fertile eggs:** Chicks hatched divided by fertile eggs, multiplied by 100.
- **Parent stock:** Breeding birds that produce offspring for a commercial production chain.
- **Floor egg:** An egg laid outside the designated nest area.
- **Traceability:** The ability to identify the source and handling history of an egg batch.
