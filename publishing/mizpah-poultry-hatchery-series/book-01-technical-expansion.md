## 13. The biology behind a fertile hatching egg

A hatching egg is the outcome of a reproductive process involving both the female and male. Understanding the broad biology helps farm teams recognise why bird condition, mating effectiveness and egg handling matter.

### 13.1 The hen's reproductive system

In the domestic hen, the functional ovary releases the yolk (ovum). As it moves through the oviduct, albumen, shell membranes and shell are added in sequence. Fertilisation, when it occurs, takes place in the upper part of the oviduct shortly after ovulation. The egg then continues through the reproductive tract before laying.

The shell is not a protective coating that can be repaired after laying. Cracks and severe shell defects can compromise the egg's suitability for incubation. Internal egg quality is also influenced by bird health, nutrition, age and management before the egg is laid.

### 13.2 The male's contribution

Sperm are produced in the testes and transferred to the female during successful mating. Hens can store sperm in specialised storage sites in the reproductive tract, allowing fertilisation of subsequent ova after mating. The duration and effectiveness of sperm storage vary; a farm should not use one assumed interval as proof that a female remains fertile.

Monitor actual fertility results rather than assuming that the presence of males guarantees fertilisation. Changes in male activity, health, body condition, age distribution, mating behaviour or flock composition should be compared with fertility trends.

### 13.3 Fertility is not embryo survival

Fertilisation is the beginning of embryonic development, not a guarantee of a live chick. An egg can be fertilised and the embryo can die early, midway through incubation or close to hatch. If a hatchery classifies every egg without a visible embryo as infertile, early embryonic death may be misclassified and the breeder farm may be blamed incorrectly.

Breakout analysis must use a consistent method and trained personnel. Where the distinction is uncertain, record the finding as uncertain or seek a second assessment rather than forcing it into a convenient category.

## 14. Breeding programme design: match the system to the objective

A breeder flock should be designed around a defined production objective, not simply around the number of birds that can fit in a house.

### 14.1 Pure-line, parent-stock and farm breeding

Commercial poultry supply chains may involve several genetic multiplication stages. A commercial hatchery commonly receives eggs from a parent-stock flock, but smallholder and local breeding systems may use a simpler arrangement. The terms used by suppliers matter: ask whether birds are commercial stock, parent stock, grandparent stock, indigenous breeding birds or mixed-origin birds.

Do not advertise offspring as a particular commercial strain unless the source and breeding status support that claim. Maintain supplier records, delivery documentation and flock identity to protect traceability and customer confidence.

### 14.2 Natural mating and artificial insemination

Natural mating is widely used in many chicken breeder systems. Other species and specialised breeding programmes may use artificial insemination. The choice depends on species, genetics, scale, housing, labour skills, welfare considerations and technical support.

Artificial insemination requires trained operators, hygienic equipment, semen handling procedures and a species-specific protocol. It should not be improvised from a general chicken instruction for use in turkeys, ducks or other birds. Farms considering it should obtain training and veterinary or breeding-specialist oversight.

### 14.3 Flock identity and replacement planning

Every breeder flock should have a unique identity. At minimum, record source, strain or type, hatch date or age, placement date, house, sex composition, health documentation and any movement between locations. Replacement birds should be introduced under a documented health and biosecurity procedure.

Mixing birds of unknown age or source can make fertility analysis and disease investigation difficult. It can also compromise the accuracy of claims made to chick buyers.

## 15. Male and female management as one reproductive system

Male and female management should be evaluated together. A flock can have apparently healthy females but poor fertility if males are underconditioned, injured, excessively heavy, socially excluded or not mating effectively. Conversely, excessive male aggression can injure females and damage welfare.

### 15.1 Observe behaviour, not only numbers

A weekly head count cannot show whether mating is taking place effectively. Staff should observe flock behaviour at suitable times and record patterns such as:
- males that are inactive or consistently isolated;
- males with impaired walking, foot lesions or injuries;
- repeated aggression or avoidance;
- females with signs of injury or feather damage;
- uneven distribution of birds around feeders, drinkers or nests;
- changes in mating activity after management adjustments.

Observation must be carried out calmly and consistently. A single brief visit may not represent the flock's normal behaviour.

### 15.2 Sex composition is not a universal fixed ratio

A ratio copied from another farm may be unsuitable because the correct programme depends on species, genotype, age, mating method, housing and male condition. Follow the current breeder guide and record any planned changes. Do not add males simply because fertility has fallen until other causes—including male quality, maturity, health and mating effectiveness—have been assessed.

### 15.3 Separate male and female monitoring

Record male and female body weights separately where the breeder programme requires it. Track mortality, removals, culls, replacements and the number of active males. Review these records alongside fertility by flock age and house. This allows the manager to distinguish a broad flock problem from one concentrated in a particular house or age group.

## 16. Nutrition, water and environmental management

Nutrition supports maintenance, growth, reproduction and egg formation. Breeder diets must be appropriate to species, strain, age and production stage. The quantity and composition of feed, feeding access, body condition and water availability should be monitored as a connected system.

### 16.1 Avoid generic feed prescriptions

A feed formula or daily allocation designed for one strain or stage should not be transferred to another flock without technical review. Energy, amino acids, minerals, vitamins and other nutrients have different roles, and the correct formulation depends on the production system and feed ingredients.

For farms mixing their own feed, quality assurance becomes especially important. Use reliable ingredient specifications, accurate weighing, appropriate mixing equipment and periodic checks. Investigate ingredient changes, storage conditions, mould, rancidity and contamination. Consult a poultry nutritionist when correcting deficiencies or reformulating diets.

### 16.2 Water is a production input

Water must be available in sufficient quantity and at suitable quality. Monitor water consumption, line function, drinker height and cleanliness, leaks and water source. Sudden changes in consumption may reflect equipment failure, temperature changes, feed changes or health problems. Test water quality when source, taste, colour, odour or flock performance raises concern.

### 16.3 Heat stress and tropical production

In hot climates, heat load can reduce feed intake, alter water consumption, affect bird behaviour and compromise production. The actual response depends on humidity, air speed, stocking density, house design, bird age and acclimatisation. A farm in a humid tropical region should not assume that the same cooling strategy used in a dry climate will work equally well.

Practical controls include maintaining ventilation systems and backup power; checking that air inlets and fans are not obstructed; providing reliable clean drinking water; monitoring bird distribution and panting; avoiding unnecessary handling during the hottest periods; maintaining shade and appropriate building insulation where applicable; and preparing an emergency response for fan or power failure.

These are general management considerations, not a substitute for a locally designed ventilation and heat-stress plan.

## 17. Biosecurity and health protection at breeder-farm level

A breeder farm supplies the next stage of the poultry production chain. A disease event can affect bird welfare, fertility, egg quality, hatchery operations and customer confidence. Biosecurity should be a daily system rather than a sign at the gate.

### 17.1 Build a written biosecurity plan

The plan should identify:
- the person responsible for biosecurity;
- approved entry points and visitor authorisation;
- vehicle and equipment movement controls;
- staff clothing, footwear and hand hygiene;
- cleaning and disinfection procedures;
- pest and wild-bird control;
- feed and water protection;
- carcass and waste disposal;
- flock separation and age-group management;
- disease reporting and veterinary escalation;
- procedures for receiving new birds and returning equipment.

The correct disinfectant, concentration, contact time and application method depend on the product label, target organism, surface and local regulations. Never mix chemicals unless the manufacturer explicitly permits it. Staff should use required personal protective equipment and follow safety data instructions.

### 17.2 Vaccination and veterinary decisions

Vaccination programmes are specific to location, disease risk, flock source, species, production system and applicable regulation. They should be developed or approved by a poultry veterinarian. This book does not prescribe a universal vaccine schedule or medication programme.

Record vaccine name, batch or lot, expiry, administration date, route, responsible person and any observed reaction. Keep veterinary prescriptions and treatment records. Observe withdrawal periods and legal restrictions where applicable, even when eggs are intended for hatching rather than human consumption.

### 17.3 Responding to a suspected disease event

If unusual mortality, respiratory signs, neurological signs, sudden production decline or other concerning symptoms occur:
1. Protect people and birds; avoid unnecessary movement.
2. Contact the flock veterinarian or relevant animal-health authority promptly.
3. Follow official reporting and sampling instructions.
4. Restrict visitors, equipment and bird movement as directed.
5. Preserve accurate mortality, production and movement records.
6. Do not sell or move birds or eggs in a way that could spread disease contrary to veterinary or regulatory advice.

Do not diagnose a disease from a single symptom or use unprescribed antibiotics as a substitute for investigation.

## 18. Egg collection, identification and the farm-to-hatchery handover

Collection staff are the first quality checkpoint. Their task is to preserve identity and prevent avoidable damage or contamination before eggs reach the grading and storage team.

### 18.1 Use a traceable batch code

A useful batch code should identify the farm, flock or house, collection date and sequence. The format is not important as long as it is unique, legible and used consistently.

The code should follow the eggs through collection, storage, transport, receiving, setting and hatch reporting. If eggs from different flocks or collection dates are combined, record the combination explicitly. Never rely on a worker's memory to reconstruct the source later.

### 18.2 Handover information

The dispatch note should include supplier and farm identity; flock and house identity; species and strain/type; collection date or date range; quantity and tray count; separate counts for any categories handled under a distinct policy; dispatch date and time; vehicle or carrier details where relevant; destination hatchery; responsible person; and unusual events or handling notes.

The receiving hatchery should acknowledge quantities and report discrepancies. A simple signed handover can prevent disputes and help identify where losses occurred.

### 18.3 Transport is part of egg management

Eggs should be protected from shock, vibration, direct sun, rain, contamination and uncontrolled temperature changes during loading and transport. Packaging, vehicle conditions, journey time and road quality all matter. The appropriate temperature and humidity targets belong in the egg-storage and handling book and must be based on species, storage duration and current technical references.

For long or rough journeys, evaluate packaging and route conditions. Avoid placing trays where they can slide, fall or be struck by other cargo. Clean and disinfect reusable transport equipment under a written procedure.

## 19. Performance review: turn records into management decisions

Records become valuable when managers review them and take proportionate action. A weekly dashboard should show trends by flock, house and batch, not only a farm-wide total.

### 19.1 Useful indicators

| Indicator | Suggested definition / calculation |
|---|---|
| Hen-day egg production | Eggs produced in the period divided by live hens present for that day, multiplied by 100; define whether floor and rejected eggs are included |
| Eggs per hen housed | Total eggs produced over a defined period divided by original number of hens placed |
| Rejection rate | Eggs rejected under the farm's stated criteria divided by eggs assessed, multiplied by 100 |
| Fertility | Fertile eggs identified divided by eggs examined, multiplied by 100 |
| Hatchability of eggs set | Chicks hatched divided by eggs set, multiplied by 100 |
| Hatchability of fertile eggs | Chicks hatched divided by fertile eggs identified, multiplied by 100 |
| Saleable chick rate | Saleable chicks divided by eggs set, multiplied by 100, if this is the agreed definition |

Do not compare farms or hatcheries unless definitions, sampling methods and time periods are comparable. State whether figures are based on all eggs, nest eggs only, eggs set, fertile eggs, or saleable chicks.

### 19.2 Use a trend, not a single number

A single day's fertility or egg production can be affected by sampling, recording errors or unusual events. Look for sustained changes and compare like with like: same strain, similar flock age, comparable season, similar egg storage and incubation conditions. Where possible, compare houses within the same farm and batches from the same source.

### 19.3 A simple monthly review meeting

The farm manager, breeder supervisor, hatchery representative and veterinarian or technical adviser should review egg production and rejection trends; fertility and hatchability feedback; male and female condition; mortality and health events; feed and water records; environmental and equipment issues; customer complaints or chick-quality feedback; and corrective actions.

Record decisions, responsible persons and due dates. Review unfinished actions at the next meeting.

## 20. Worked farm-to-hatchery example

The following example demonstrates how records can reveal where a problem may sit. All figures are illustrative and are not industry targets.

A breeder farm dispatches 4,000 eggs from one identified flock over a documented collection period. The hatchery receives 3,960 eggs in acceptable condition after recording 40 damaged eggs at receipt. Of the 3,960 accepted eggs, 3,800 are set after the hatchery's own grading. The hatchery later records 3,420 chicks hatched.

The farm and hatchery should reconcile dispatched quantity against received quantity; received quantity against eggs accepted and eggs set; egg rejection categories and timing; source flock and house identity; storage and transport history; fertility sampling method and sample size; and incubation and hatch records.

This example demonstrates why each stage needs its own denominator. The difference between eggs dispatched, received, accepted and set is operationally important. A fertility sample should not be treated as an exact count of every egg unless the entire batch has been examined using an appropriate method.

## 22. Case study: fertility falls in one house

A farm operates several breeder houses. Hatchery feedback shows a decline in fertility from eggs sourced from one house, while other houses remain broadly stable. The manager initially proposes changing the male ratio across the entire farm.

A better response is to isolate the affected house and compare male and female numbers and age profile; male body condition, mobility and mating behaviour; recent male removals or introductions; feed and water access; house temperature, ventilation and lighting records; flock health and veterinary events; egg collection and batch identification; and hatchery sampling and breakout records.

If the evidence points to a house-specific male-management issue, a targeted intervention can be planned with the breeder technical adviser. If the evidence instead shows a sampling or traceability error, the farm avoids an unnecessary flock-wide change. The lesson is to diagnose at the smallest reliable level before changing the whole operation.

## 23. Case study: more floor eggs and dirty eggs

A farm observes a rising number of floor eggs and dirty eggs. Staff suggest that the flock has become less productive. The manager separates production volume from egg quality and reviews the records.

The investigation finds that the increase coincided with a damaged nest section and a change in litter condition. The farm repairs the nest equipment, restores the agreed litter-management routine, retrains collection staff and records nest versus floor eggs separately. The result is assessed over subsequent comparable periods rather than declared successful after one day.

The case illustrates how operational records can connect an observed egg-quality problem with a practical housing or workflow cause.

## 24. Reader's action plan

Before the next breeder-farm review, the reader should be able to answer:

1. Can every hatching-egg batch be traced to a specific flock and house?
2. Is the breeder strain or genetic type known and supported by supplier documentation?
3. Are current strain-specific management guides available to staff?
4. Are male and female condition monitored separately where required?
5. Are feed, water, health, mortality and egg-production records complete?
6. Are nest eggs and floor eggs recorded separately?
7. Are rejected eggs categorised rather than recorded as one unexplained total?
8. Does the hatchery report fertility and hatchability using clear definitions?
9. Are transport and handover records complete?
10. Are corrective actions assigned, dated and reviewed?

A breeder farm does not need expensive software to begin improving control. It needs consistent definitions, reliable records, trained staff and management that responds to evidence.
