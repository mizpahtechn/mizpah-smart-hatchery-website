Chapter 1: Set up a finance rhythm

A small business needs timely records and a regular review cycle more than complex software. Define who records transactions, who approves payments, who reconciles accounts and who reviews results.

Use daily sales and cash checks, weekly receivables and cash forecasts, and monthly management accounts. Set deadlines and a named owner for each task.

Chapter 2: Build a driver-based budget

Start with business drivers: units sold, average price, customer visits, staff hours, production capacity, delivery cost and supplier prices. Build revenue and cost forecasts from those drivers rather than applying arbitrary growth percentages.

Prepare a base case and a downside case. Record assumptions, source, owner and review date. Update the budget when actual evidence changes materially.

Chapter 3: Understand margins and break-even

Calculate gross profit, contribution, operating profit and net profit consistently. Contribution per unit is price less variable cost. Break-even volume is fixed costs divided by contribution per unit, assuming the unit economics and cost structure remain stable.

Test the effect of discounts, returns, waste, payment fees and delivery subsidies. A higher sales volume can worsen cash or profit if each order has negative contribution.

Chapter 4: Control cash and working capital

Forecast receipts and payments by date, not merely by accounting month. Monitor customer collection days, supplier payment terms, inventory days and seasonal cash needs.

Use credit checks and limits proportionate to risk. Reorder stock using demand and lead-time evidence. Negotiate supplier terms responsibly and avoid financing long-lived assets with short-term obligations where possible.

Chapter 5: Manage costs and procurement

Classify costs by purpose and behaviour. Review recurring subscriptions, energy, logistics, staffing and waste. Compare suppliers on total cost, reliability, quality, payment terms and concentration risk—not unit price alone.

Use purchase requests, approval thresholds, purchase orders, goods-received checks and invoice matching. Document exceptions and review related-party transactions transparently.

Chapter 6: Build internal financial controls

Separate duties where possible: a person requesting a payment should not be the only person approving and reconciling it. Use named accounts, multi-factor authentication, transaction alerts and verified supplier details.

Reconcile bank, wallet, cash, inventory and payment-provider settlements. Investigate unusual refunds, duplicate invoices, unexplained adjustments and changes to beneficiary accounts.

Chapter 7: Prepare for tax, lending and investment

Keep complete records of sales, expenses, payroll, assets, liabilities and tax filings. Requirements vary by jurisdiction, entity type and activity; confirm current rules with the relevant authority or qualified professional.

For borrowing, understand interest, fees, collateral, covenants, repayment schedule and default consequences. Stress-test debt service under lower sales and delayed collections. Do not sign terms you cannot explain.

Chapter 8: Use finance to decide and improve

Compare actuals with budget, investigate material variances and assign corrective action. Use return on investment, payback and cash impact alongside strategic and operational considerations.

A monthly dashboard should include revenue, gross margin, operating costs, cash runway, overdue receivables, inventory ageing, debt obligations and forecast variance. Make decisions from reconciled data, not guesswork.

Your implementation plan

  1. Define the result you want and the baseline you will measure.
  2. Gather current evidence and identify assumptions that need testing.
  3. Choose a small, time-bound experiment with a clear owner and budget.
  4. Review results, customer feedback, cash impact and risks.
  5. Document the decision, update the process and set the next review date.
Practical worksheet: record the objective, action, responsible person, resources, deadline, success measure, evidence and contingency.
