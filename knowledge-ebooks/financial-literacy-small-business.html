Financial Literacy for Small Business Owners

Understand business money, records, cash flow, pricing, working capital and financial decisions with practical tools.

Chapter 1: Separate business and personal money

Use a dedicated business account or wallet where available and appropriate. Record owner contributions, drawings, reimbursements and business expenses separately. Mixing funds makes it difficult to know whether the business is profitable or simply using personal cash.

Set a documented owner-pay policy. Keep receipts and invoices, reconcile transactions and restrict access to payment credentials. Never share one-time passwords or banking PINs with staff or advisers.

Chapter 2: Understand the three core reports

A profit-and-loss statement summarises income and expenses over a period. A balance sheet shows assets, liabilities and equity at a point in time. A cash-flow statement explains cash received and paid. Each answers a different question.

Profit is not the same as cash. Credit sales can increase reported revenue before money arrives; buying inventory can use cash before the goods are sold. Review all three views rather than judging the business from the bank balance alone.

Chapter 3: Build reliable records

Record each sale, purchase, expense, debt, stock movement and owner transaction promptly. Use consistent categories and keep source documents. Correct errors with traceable adjustments rather than deleting history.

At least monthly, reconcile bank and wallet balances, payment-provider settlements, customer debts, supplier balances and physical stock. Investigate differences and document the resolution.

Chapter 4: Know costs and contribution

Separate fixed costs that remain broadly stable from variable costs that change with each sale. Include direct materials, packaging, transaction fees, delivery, wastage and direct labour when calculating unit economics.

Contribution per unit equals selling price less variable cost per unit. It contributes toward fixed costs and profit. Do not confuse markup with margin: markup is based on cost, while margin is based on selling price.

Chapter 5: Prepare a cash forecast

Prepare a rolling 13-week forecast. Start with opening cash, list realistic receipts by expected date, list payments by due date and calculate closing cash each week. Include tax, loan payments, wages, rent and seasonal costs.

Create base, downside and upside scenarios. If a shortfall appears, act early: collect overdue invoices, negotiate supplier timing, reduce discretionary spending or arrange appropriate finance. Do not count unapproved loans or uncertain sales as cash.

Chapter 6: Manage working capital and stock

Working capital supports day-to-day operations. Too much stock or slow customer payment can trap cash; too little stock can cause lost sales. Set reorder points based on demand, supplier lead time and safety stock.

Review ageing inventory, slow-moving items, customer credit limits and overdue balances. Agree payment terms before delivery and follow up professionally. Avoid extending credit simply to make reported sales look higher.

Chapter 7: Budget, tax and controls

Prepare a budget based on operating assumptions, then compare actual results with the plan. Investigate material variances and update forecasts when conditions change. A budget is a decision tool, not a promise that results will occur.

Maintain approval limits, separate payment preparation from approval where possible and verify supplier bank-detail changes independently. Keep tax records and seek qualified local advice on registration, filing, payroll and statutory duties.

Chapter 8: Make better financial decisions

Evaluate equipment, hiring and expansion by estimating total cost, expected benefit, cash timing, useful life and risks. Compare alternatives, including delaying or outsourcing. Use conservative assumptions and test whether the business can withstand lower sales.

A monthly finance meeting can ask: What did we earn? What cash came in? What is owed? Which costs changed? What is the next cash risk? What decision must be made? Record the decision, owner and review date.

Your 30-day action plan

  1. Days 1–3: establish your goal, baseline and constraints.
  2. Days 4–7: gather evidence from customers, records or existing processes.
  3. Week 2: run one small, measurable experiment.
  4. Week 3: review results, costs, feedback and operational issues.
  5. Week 4: document lessons and choose the next improvement.
Working tool: keep a weekly record of the action, owner, due date, evidence, result and next decision.
